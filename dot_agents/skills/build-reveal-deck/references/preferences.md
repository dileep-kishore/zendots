# The user's standing asks for decks

Collected from the user's feedback on past talks. Each item caused rework when
it was missed.

## How the deck is presented

- The user presents by sharing a browser tab and does not read speaker notes.
  Anything the audience must hear goes on the slide, as a short takeaway
  bullet or a fragment. Notes are optional background only.
- Use a light theme with smooth transitions and animations: a slide
  transition, staggered entrances, and boxes and arrows that build with clicks.
- Ten to twelve slides for a short talk, unless the user asks otherwise.
- Store decks in `/Volumes/WorkSSD/Work/Presentations/<Topic>_<YYYY>_<Mon>/`.
  That folder syncs between machines, so keep scratch files and screenshots
  out of it.

## What a slide looks like

- One title line at the top that states a claim. One core visual takes most
  of the slide. Use one or two takeaway bullets only when they add something.
- Diagrams must be accurate. Show the real pipeline, including its branches.
  For example, routine results pass automatic checks, and only new claims wait
  for a person. Add nuance rather than simplify into something wrong.
- A preview or mock of the proposed product beats an abstract box diagram.
- Mark mock content as illustrative and proposals as proposed.
- Prefer a real example with checked numbers over an invented one. Check every
  number against the source file. Put a small source line on slides with
  numbers.
- Keep it high level. Leave out task-level detail such as identifiers, change
  notices or field lists, and add no timelines unless asked.

## What the argument needs

- Propose something concrete. Say what it is, how agents use it, how people
  use it, and how it stays current. A vague "put everything in a new layer"
  answer was rejected.
- Answer the room's obvious objection on a slide. For example: "If the
  Clearinghouse stores the results, why a separate knowledge base?"
- When a meeting agenda exists, the deck must serve its stated objectives.
  Use the example the agenda names, such as annotation.
- Claims about other tools go stale fast. Check them against current primary
  sources before they go on a slide. Describe gaps in our own systems, from
  their own docs, rather than in someone else's product.
- The summary slide covers what we have (building blocks), the end product,
  and how we get there, in plain words. Drop generic sections such as "How we
  work together", and any bullet that sounds like filler.
- End on the decision or help you need from the room.

## People and branding

- Name no individual people on slides or in notes. Credit the system, team or
  meeting instead: "the Clearinghouse team", "Monday's summit". The
  presenter's name on the title slide is the only exception.
- Match the affiliation and branding to the host. A KBase talk gets the KBase
  template and no BERIL tag next to the presenter's name.
- Follow the global writing rules on slides too: no em dashes, plain words,
  one idea per sentence, no bold-label lists.

## How to work

- Back up the deck before a theme overhaul or a large rewrite.
- Inspect the rendered slides yourself after each round of changes. A code
  review does not catch arrows that appear too early, labels sitting on lines,
  tiny logos or cramped headers.
- Consult the other vendor's model at each stage (outline, design, finished
  screenshots). Fix what it confirms, and drop findings it cannot support.
- When the user only asks whether the deck is ready, answer with what is
  verified and what still blocks them. Do not start new edits.
